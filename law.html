01 · BASIC RIGHTS AT SCHOOL

BASIC RIGHTS AT SCHOOL.

Students have rights at school, including protections involving speech and expression, privacy, fair treatment, and certain disciplinary procedures. Knowing what those rights are and where they may have limits can help you understand what your school can and cannot do.

01 SEARCHES +

The Fourth Amendment protects students from unreasonable searches, but this protection works differently in the school environment because of the need to maintain a safe place where students can also learn.

Schools can conduct certain searches to maintain safety and order without needing a warrant or probable cause. In public schools, school officials generally need reasonable suspicion that a student has violated a law or school rule, and the search must be reasonably related to what they are investigating and not excessively intrusive.

Police generally have a higher standard, usually requiring probable cause to conduct a search.

02 DISCIPLINE +

Schools can discipline students for violating school rules or laws, but public schools must still follow certain legal protections when imposing serious discipline.

For example, students facing a suspension of 10 days or less generally must receive notice of the allegations and an opportunity to explain their side before or promptly after the suspension, depending on the circumstances.

The exact rules for discipline, longer suspensions, expulsions, and appeals can vary by state and school district, and discipline cannot be administered in a way that unlawfully discriminates against students.

03 DRESS CODE +

There is no single federal dress code policy for schools in the United States. Instead, dress codes are set locally by individual school districts or school boards.

While policies vary widely, public schools must follow federal and state constitutional laws ensuring rules do not unlawfully discriminate based on gender, race, or religion.

02 · LAW BASICS

UNDERSTAND THE LAW.

The United States is a democracy, meaning people have a role in how their government is shaped and how decisions are made. Understanding the basics of government, laws, rights, and freedoms can help you better understand the systems that affect your everyday life.

01 THE PROCESS OF A LAW +

Laws generally move through a series of steps before they become law.

01

AN IDEA

A law usually starts with an idea about a problem that someone wants the government to address. The idea can come from lawmakers, organizations, communities, or members of the public.

02

THE IDEA BECOMES A BILL

A bill is a proposed law. A lawmaker writes the idea into a formal proposal that can be introduced for consideration.

03

INTRODUCTION

The bill is formally introduced in the legislature. At the federal level, this means it is introduced in either the House of Representatives or the Senate.

04

COMMITTEE

The bill is usually sent to a committee that specializes in its subject. The committee may study it, hold hearings, make changes, and decide whether it should move forward.

05

DEBATE & VOTE

If the bill moves forward, lawmakers debate it and may propose changes. The members of that chamber then vote on whether to pass it.

06

THE OTHER CHAMBER

In a two-chamber legislature, the bill generally has to go through a similar process in the other chamber. Both chambers must ultimately agree on the same version of the bill.

07

GOVERNOR / PRESIDENT

Once the legislature passes the bill, it goes to the executive, such as a governor at the state level or the president at the federal level. The executive may sign it into law or, depending on the government and circumstances, veto it.

08

IT BECOMES LAW

If the bill is approved through the required process, it becomes a law and can be put into effect according to its terms. Government agencies and officials may then be responsible for enforcing or carrying out the law.

02 WHAT IS FREEDOM OF SPEECH? +

We often hear people say, "I have freedom of speech," but what does that actually mean?

The First Amendment says that the government may not abridge the freedom of speech, protecting people's ability to express ideas and opinions.

But it does not mean that every kind of speech is protected in every situation or that private individuals and organizations must allow every form of expression.

In simple terms, freedom of speech protects you from certain government restrictions on your expression, while some forms of speech and conduct may have legal limits.

03 DIFFERENT TYPES OF LAWS +
01

LOCAL LAWS

Local laws are rules made by local governments, such as cities, towns, and counties, to address issues within their communities. They are usually created by local governing bodies, such as city councils or town boards. Local laws generally apply only within that particular community or jurisdiction.

02

STATE LAWS

State laws are laws created by a state's government that apply throughout that state. They are generally made by the state legislature and can be signed or vetoed by the governor. State laws can cover areas such as education, transportation, public safety, and many other issues.

03

FEDERAL LAWS

Federal laws are laws made by the United States government and generally apply across the country. They are created by Congress, which consists of the House of Representatives and the Senate, and are generally sent to the President after passing both chambers. Federal laws cover matters within the authority given to the federal government.

03 · THE COURTS

HOW COURTS WORK.

A court is a place where legal cases are heard and decided according to the law. Courts can resolve disputes, determine whether someone is responsible for a crime or other legal violation, and review certain decisions made by lower courts.

01 STATE VS. FEDERAL COURTS +

The United States has both state and federal court systems, and each handles different types of cases.

STATE

STATE COURTS

State courts generally handle matters involving state laws and most legal cases.

VS.
FEDERAL

FEDERAL COURTS

Federal courts handle cases involving federal laws, the U.S. Constitution, and other matters within federal jurisdiction.

Both systems have trial and appellate courts, but their structures, rules, and the types of cases they can hear can differ.

02 JUDGES, ATTORNEYS, JURIES & PARTIES +

JUDGES

Judges oversee court proceedings, interpret and apply the law, and make decisions within their authority.

ATTORNEYS

Attorneys are lawyers who represent and advise the people or organizations involved in a case.

JURIES

Juries are groups of citizens who may decide facts and reach a verdict in certain trials.

PARTIES

The parties are the people, organizations, or governments directly involved in a case. For example, a plaintiff and defendant in a civil case or the government and a defendant in a criminal case.

04 APPELLATE COURT

A higher court reviews legal issues.

Trial courts are where cases are initially heard, evidence is presented, witnesses may testify, and the facts of a case are determined.

If a party believes a legal error or unfair procedure affected the decision, they may be able to appeal to a higher court.

Appeals courts generally review whether the law was applied correctly and whether the proceedings were fair rather than holding a completely new trial or hearing new evidence.
