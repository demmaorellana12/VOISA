THE PEOPLE'S SOUND

Music is one of the few things capable of reaching us before we have found the words to explain ourselves. It can regulate us, motivate us, comfort us, challenge us, and reach the most vulnerable parts of who we are.

It has always been more than entertainment. From the earliest parts of human history, people have used music to preserve memory, tell stories, express grief, celebrate identity, resist injustice, and send messages that words alone could not carry.

Every generation leaves something behind in its music.

THE SONG OF THE MONTH

Music carries history. Across generations, songs have reflected the world around them — sometimes celebrating it, sometimes mourning it, and sometimes demanding that it change.

Each month, we choose one piece of music and look beyond the sound itself. What was happening when it was created? Who heard it? What did it mean to them? What movement, conflict, culture, or moment shaped it?

What can a song tell us about the people who made it?

September 2026 MONTH 002

Zombie

COMPOSER / ARTIST The Cranberries
YEAR 1994
GENRE Alternative Rock
HISTORICAL CONTEXT

In 1993, Northern Ireland and the United Kingdom were still living through the Troubles, a decades-long conflict shaped by political, national, and religious divisions. Violence had extended far beyond Northern Ireland, with bombings and attacks also taking place in England. On March 20, two bombs exploded on Bridge Street in Warrington, Cheshire, killing three-year-old Johnathan Ball and, five days later, twelve-year-old Tim Parry, while injuring dozens of others. The deaths of two children sparked widespread grief and public anger, becoming another painful reminder that political violence was not experienced only by governments or armed groups, but by ordinary people and families. Later that year, while The Cranberries were touring England, lead singer Dolores O’Riordan began writing a song that would confront that violence directly. “Zombie” was not written as a celebration of one side of the conflict. It emerged from grief, frustration, and a question about what happens when generations continue to inherit violence.

Created / performed by: The Cranberries
Composer: Dolores O’Riordan · Producer: Stephen Street · Writer: Dolores O’Riordan· Musicians: Dolores O’Riordan · Noel Hogan · Mike Hogan · Fergal Lawler

VOISA · THE PEOPLE'S SOUND
THE STORY BEHIND THE SONG

“Zombie” did not begin as a hit song. It began with a tragedy. On March 20, 1993, two bombs exploded in Warrington, England, during the years-long conflict known as the Troubles. Two children, three-year-old Johnathan Ball and twelve-year-old Tim Parry, were killed, and dozens of others were injured. The bombing was attributed to the Provisional Irish Republican Army, whose armed campaign sought to end British rule in Northern Ireland and bring about a united Ireland. The Troubles had already taken thousands of lives, but what struck Dolores O’Riordan was the innocence of the victims. The Cranberries were touring England at the time, and the attack stayed with her. Years later, she recalled how difficult it was to be Irish in Britain while tensions were so high, especially when violence was being carried out in the name of Ireland. Her reaction was not to write a political manifesto. It was to write about the human cost of political violence, and about children who had nothing to do with the conflict becoming its victims. The song came to O’Riordan in 1993, midway through the Cranberries’ English tour. She began with a few chords on an acoustic guitar, almost without knowing where they were going. Back in Limerick, she continued developing the idea, quickly building the chorus and verses around those chords. At first, the song was called “In Your Head.” When she brought it into the Cranberries’ rehearsal space in Mungret, however, it sounded like the band’s earlier work: softer guitars, melodic arrangements, the kind of indie-pop sound audiences already associated with songs such as “Linger” and “Dreams.” O’Riordan wanted something completely different. She picked up an electric guitar and pushed the song into a heavier direction, matching the anger underneath the lyrics. The band followed her lead. Drummer Fergal Lawler later recalled that O’Riordan wanted more distortion and greater force from the drums, while guitarist Noel Hogan helped build the layers of guitar underneath her riffs. The result was a turning point. The Cranberries had discovered that they could keep their melodies while making their music much louder, darker, and more aggressive.

The band knew “Zombie” was different, and audiences knew it too. The Cranberries began performing it live long before it was recorded, giving the song nearly a year to grow in front of crowds. The reaction convinced the band that this was not simply another album track. They wanted it to become the lead single from their second album, No Need to Argue, even though their record label was reportedly uneasy about releasing such a politically charged song. The Cranberries had already built an international audience around songs that were melodic and romantic, while “Zombie” was loud, angry, and directly connected to violence in Ireland and Britain. According to former manager Allen Kovac, Island Records encouraged the band to consider another song instead. O’Riordan refused. She believed that if the song expressed what she genuinely felt, changing it simply because it might be controversial would defeat its purpose. Eventually, the label relented. “Zombie” was released on September 19, 1994, and the song that had once seemed too political for radio became one of the defining songs of the Cranberries’ career. It reached number one on the Alternative Airplay chart in the United States and topped charts across several countries, while its success helped propel No Need to Argue into international stardom. But the controversy did not disappear when the song became successful. If anything, its success made the questions surrounding it louder. “Zombie” was unmistakably about political violence, but O’Riordan was not from Northern Ireland. She was from Limerick in the Republic of Ireland, and some critics questioned whether she could speak meaningfully about a conflict with such a complicated history. Others argued that the song reduced a deeply complicated political conflict to a simple expression of grief and anger. Those criticisms are part of the song’s history, and they reveal something important about protest music: a song can come from a sincere place while still being interpreted differently by people who have lived through the history it describes. O’Riordan herself emphasized that she was not speaking for the IRA, nor presenting the violence as representative of Irish people. Her focus was the human consequence of it. The song’s refrain insists on separation from the violence, while the repeated question of what remains “in your head” turns its attention toward the way conflict can survive through memory, resentment, and inherited divisions. Even the title, “Zombie,” has no single confirmed explanation from O’Riordan. Writers have interpreted it in different ways, from a metaphor for people trapped in old grievances to an image of the dead continuing to occupy the minds of the living. Musically, that uncertainty is part of what gives the song its force. “Zombie” was often described as alternative rock or grunge, but O’Riordan later explained that the heavier sound was not an attempt to imitate the grunge movement. It was simply the sound that felt right for the anger she was trying to express. Her voice became another instrument of that anger. Instead of singing the lyrics with the polished restraint of a conventional pop performance, she pushed her Irish accent, pitch changes, and distinctive vocal breaks to the edge. The verses feel controlled; the chorus erupts. The contrast mirrors the subject itself: beneath the familiar melody is something unresolved.

Then came the music video. Directed by Samuel Bayer, it placed the Cranberries’ performance inside imagery connected directly to Northern Ireland. O’Riordan appears covered in gold paint before a large cross, surrounded by children painted silver, while black-and-white footage shows Belfast, murals, children, and British soldiers. The video deliberately places beauty beside images of conflict, creating a visual version of the song’s central question: how can people continue to live ordinary lives while violence and history surround them? The original video was controversial enough that both the BBC and Ireland’s RTÉ restricted it because of its imagery, instead airing an edited performance-focused version. Yet the original endured. Decades later, it became one of the most recognizable rock videos of the 1990s, passing one billion views on YouTube in April 2020, making “Zombie” the first song by an Irish group to reach that milestone. What makes “Zombie” unusual is that its story did not end with the 1990s. O’Riordan continued performing it throughout the Cranberries’ career, including stripped-down versions that revealed how much of the song’s power could survive without the enormous guitars. After her death in 2018, the song took on another life. It became associated with sporting crowds in Limerick and later with Ireland’s national rugby team, where supporters began singing it together after matches during the 2023 Rugby World Cup. That transformation has itself been debated, because a song written in mourning for children killed in political violence was now being sung collectively as a national sporting anthem. For some, that represents a rejection of violence and a remembrance of the victims; for others, the song’s connection to the Troubles makes its use more politically complicated. The debate is almost strangely fitting. “Zombie” has always been a song about what happens when history refuses to stay in the past. And perhaps the most unexpected chapter came after O’Riordan was gone. In January 2018, the American hard-rock band Bad Wolves prepared a cover of “Zombie.” O’Riordan had heard their version and agreed to record vocals for it. She was scheduled to meet the band in a London studio on January 15, but she died that morning before the recording could take place. Bad Wolves released their version several days later and directed proceeds toward her children. A song O’Riordan had written as a young woman responding to the deaths of two children had now passed through another generation, another band, and another loss. sMore than thirty years after it was written, “Zombie” remains difficult to place neatly into one category. It is a rock song, a protest song, a memorial, a piece of Irish cultural history, and a record of one musician trying to make sense of violence that she could not accept. Its power does not come from solving the history behind the Troubles. It comes from refusing to let the human beings caught inside that history disappear behind political arguments. Dolores O’Riordan took a tragedy that happened to other people, carried it with her, and turned that grief into five minutes of music that millions of people would eventually know by heart. The song does not ask its listeners to remember a political side. It asks them to remember the cost.

-Written by Demma Orellana, Founder and Director of VOISA
